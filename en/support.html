About On Person

It keeps an eye on things for the half-minute before you leave

The phone is the easiest thing to forget on your way out. On Person does one thing: it watches the Bluetooth link between your Apple Watch and your iPhone. When the two drift apart and your watch notices first, it vibrates and shows a notification — so you turn around and pick up the phone you left behind while it's close by.

It doesn't locate you, doesn't know where you are, and doesn't talk to a server. The full boundaries are in the privacy policy, with nothing hidden.

Guarding

An instrument panel: current signal strength, loss threshold, grace remaining, last heartbeat, phone battery. Every reading is on screen — no guesswork.

Separation alert

It waits out the grace period, then tells you when your phone was last with you — “31 seconds ago it was still with you.” One tap on the wrist puts it away.

Silence policy

Six rules decide when it should stay quiet: watch charging, phone charging, quiet hours, sleep mode, quieter once you're home, and a Focus mode check-up. Anything that happens during a quiet period is still recorded.

Self-check

Background refresh, Bluetooth, Watch permission, Focus mode prompt — the four things that must hold. All on one screen, with a one-tap fix.

Common questions

Does it know where I am? ▼
No, and it doesn't want to. The signal strength it reads (RSSI) is a number from a radio: it answers “is that end still answering?”, not “which end is that?”. It doesn't request location permission, doesn't convert anything into a coordinate, and keeps no location history — the location tracking common in this category isn't in the app at all.
What do I need to use it? ▼
An iPhone and an Apple Watch that are already paired. That's it — no account, no subscription, no server to connect to. The watch app asks for notification permission once, the first time it opens. Declining doesn't break anything else; you just won't get a banner on your wrist.
Why doesn't it always fire? ▼
Four kinds of reason, none of them a malfunction:

① System Focus modes (Do Not Disturb, Sleep, Work) hold back ordinary notifications. We can't read whether Focus is on, so we can't promise it always gets through.

② The background budget. The system wakes the watch only so often (roughly every 10–15 minutes), so with the screen off a real separation can take minutes to confirm.

③ A silence rule is in effect: watch charging, phone charging, quiet hours, sleep mode, home noise suppression. In those cases it records instead of bothering you.

④ You're still in the grace period. A weak signal starts a grace period first; if the link comes back inside it, nothing happens.

The Connection self-check in the app tells you which one you're hitting.
How do I make it fire as reliably as possible? ▼
Three things, and the Connection self-check checks all of them:

① Turn on Time Sensitive in System Settings › Notifications › On Person. Only then can a notification break through Focus.

② Keep Background App Refresh on (System Settings › General › Background App Refresh).

③ Make sure your watch is connected to the iPhone and has enough battery.

If it still stays quiet after all that, you're hitting a silence rule — not a broken app.
Will it cry wolf? ▼
There are two gates. First the grace period: after the signal weakens it waits (30 seconds by default), and if the link comes back inside that window nothing happens. Second the threshold: −72 dBm by default, so the signal has to get genuinely weak. Both numbers are adjustable in Settings.

And separation isn't "any disconnect": while backgrounded, only a genuinely weak signal counts. Not hearing a broadcast at all does not (the system often suspends the watch's own scanning in the background).
How does it recover afterwards? ▼
Two ways, neither of which involves taking your phone out:

① You come back to your phone — tap “I'm back” on your wrist and it's put away immediately; or tap “Keep guarding 3 min” to reset the grace period.

② You ignore it — the Watch reconnects on its own and writes the automatic recovery into your history. So you can see that it recovered, not just that it fired.
What's in the history, and what can I see? ▼
Each entry holds four things: a random ID, the moment, the kind of event, and one short note. There are nine kinds of event (signal weak entering grace, grace ended and alert fired, recorded during a quiet period, auto-reconnect succeeded, you came back to your phone, keep guarding, started guarding, stopped guarding, make my phone ring).

No location. No signal history. No advertising identifier. Up to 50 entries, newest pushes out oldest, clearable whenever you like.
Does it need a network connection or an account? ▼
Neither. There's no account system and no server, there is no network request anywhere in the app, and no analytics SDK, crash reporter or ad SDK is linked in. The only outbound communication is the App Store purchase and restore, handled entirely by Apple's own framework.
Which languages are supported? Do the watch and complications follow? ▼
中文, English and 日本語 — pick one in Settings › Language.

It takes effect right away, and the watch face and the complications follow too: the language is pushed to the watch over WatchConnectivity, so you never have to set it a second time on the watch itself.
When exactly does the app not work? ▼
We'd rather you know the limits before you rely on them:

· Background scans get about 5 chances a day — not continuous listening.
· An alert repeats about 5 times over roughly 4 minutes; watchOS won't let apps vibrate in the background.
· Proximity is judged from the system Bluetooth stack, so a disconnect isn't caught the instant it happens.
· System Focus modes will hold back our alerts, and we can't read whether Focus is on.
· With the screen off, a real separation can take minutes to confirm.

The full section is on the “Privacy & limits” screen inside the app, and in the privacy policy.
